Chapter 0 – Introduction to Time Series

Key Concepts

A time series is a sequence of observations \(x_1, x_2, \ldots\) taken at equally spaced times. We assume:

  • Continuous numerical values.
  • Discrete, equispaced time points (often \(t=1,2,3,\dots\)).
  • Some randomness that we aim to model.

Goals: Understand (visualise), Explain (decompose), Predict (forecast), Control (adjust inputs).

0.1 Probability vs Statistics

Probability: we work with random variables (upper case \(X, Y\)) and their distributions – modelling.
Statistics: we have actual numbers \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) (lower case) and estimate parameters – inference.

Chapter 1 – The Additive Model and Trend Estimation

1.1 The Additive Model

\[ X_t = m_t + s_t + Y_t \]

\(m_t\): trend, \(s_t\): seasonal component (periodic), \(Y_t\): residual noise, \(\mathbb{E}[Y_t]=0\).

Components can be centred: if \(\frac{1}{S}\sum s_t \neq 0\), redefine \(m_t^{\text{new}} = m_t + \bar s\), \(s_t^{\text{new}} = s_t - \bar s\).

Multiplicative Model

\[ X_t = m_t \cdot s_t \cdot Y_t \quad\Rightarrow\quad \log X_t = \log m_t + \log s_t + \log Y_t \]

Use logarithms to turn multiplicative effects into additive ones.

1.2 Linear Regression for Trend

Assume \(m_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t\). Minimise sum of squared errors:

\[ S(\beta_0,\beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - (\beta_0+\beta_1 t_i))^2. \]

Normal equations lead to:

\[ \hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum (t_i-\bar t)(x_i-\bar x)}{\sum (t_i-\bar t)^2}, \qquad \hat\beta_0 = \bar x - \hat\beta_1 \bar t. \]

Worked Example 1.1 – Linear Trend

Suppose we have annual sales (in £k) for years 1 to 5: \(x = [12, 15, 18, 22, 25]\).

\(\bar t = 3,\; \bar x = 18.4\).
\(\sum (t_i-\bar t)^2 = (1-3)^2+(2-3)^2+(3-3)^2+(4-3)^2+(5-3)^2 = 4+1+0+1+4 = 10\).
\(\sum (t_i-\bar t)(x_i-\bar x) = (-2)(-6.4)+(-1)(-3.4)+(0)(-0.4)+(1)(3.6)+(2)(6.6) = 12.8+3.4+0+3.6+13.2 = 33\).
Thus \(\hat\beta_1 = 33/10 = 3.3\), \(\hat\beta_0 = 18.4 - 3.3\times 3 = 8.5\).

Fitted trend: \(m_t = 8.5 + 3.3\,t\). Predicted year 6 sales: \(8.5+3.3\times6 = 28.3\).

1.3 Polynomial Regression

Trend: \(m_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \dots + \beta_k t^k\). In matrix form:

\[ \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon,\quad \mathbf{W} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t_1 & \cdots & t_1^k\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 1 & t_n & \cdots & t_n^k \end{pmatrix},\quad \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W})^{-1}\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{X}. \]

Worked Example 1.2 – Quadratic Trend by Hand (small data)

Data: \(t=[1,2,3]\), \(x=[2, 6, 7]\). Fit \(m_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \beta_2 t^2\).

Design matrix: \[ \mathbf{W} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1 & 1\\ 1 & 2 & 4\\ 1 & 3 & 9\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\6\\7\end{pmatrix}. \] Compute \(\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W} = \begin{pmatrix}3 & 6 & 14\\ 6 & 14 & 36\\ 14 & 36 & 98\end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix}15\\ 39\\ 101\end{pmatrix}\).

Solving \((\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W})\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{X}\) gives \(\hat\beta_0 = -1,\; \hat\beta_1 = 6,\; \hat\beta_2 = -1\). So \(m_t = -1 + 6t - t^2\).

Check: \(t=1:4,\;2:7,\;3:8\)? Wait, \(t=1: -1+6-1=4\) but data is 2 – seems off. Let's recalc: 4? Actually 4 ≠ 2, so our quadratic fits imperfectly because with 3 points a quadratic interpolates exactly. The solution above should give perfect fit. Let's verify: plug t=1: -1+6-1=4; should be 2. So maybe I miscalculated. Let's solve quickly: we have matrix equation. Actually with 3 points, if we solve directly, we can set up equations: \(t=1: \beta_0+\beta_1+\beta_2 = 2\) \(t=2: \beta_0+2\beta_1+4\beta_2 = 6\) \(t=3: \beta_0+3\beta_1+9\beta_2 = 7\). Subtract first from second: \(\beta_1+3\beta_2=4\). Subtract second from third: \(\beta_1+5\beta_2=1\). Then subtract: \(2\beta_2=-3 \Rightarrow \beta_2 = -1.5\). Then \(\beta_1 = 4 - 3(-1.5)=8.5\). Then \(\beta_0 = 2 - \beta_1 - \beta_2 = 2 -8.5 +1.5 = -5\). So \(m_t = -5 + 8.5 t -1.5 t^2\). Check: t=1: -5+8.5-1.5=2; t=2: -5+17-6=6; t=3: -5+25.5-13.5=7. Perfect. So the normal equations with the computed matrices might have been wrong because of a calculation error. This illustrates the method; in practice we use R. So the trend is \(m_t = -5 + 8.5t -1.5t^2\).

Chapter 2 – Non‑parametric Trend Methods I: Moving Averages and Filters

2.1 Simple Moving Average

Odd window \(2q+1\): \(\hat m_t = \frac{1}{2q+1}\sum_{j=-q}^q x_{t-j}\)

Even window \(2q\): \(\hat m_t = \frac{1}{2q}\bigl(\frac12 x_{t-q} + x_{t-q+1} + \dots + x_{t+q-1} + \frac12 x_{t+q}\bigr)\)

Worked Example 2.1 – 3‑point Moving Average

Data: \(x = [8, 5, 9, 7, 12]\).

  • \(m_2 = (8+5+9)/3 = 7.33\)
  • \(m_3 = (5+9+7)/3 = 7.0\)
  • \(m_4 = (9+7+12)/3 = 9.33\)
  • \(m_1\) and \(m_5\) are undefined (NA).

Observe that the random bumpiness is reduced (5 became 7.33, etc.).

Exercises – Moving Averages

  1. Given \(x = [2, 6, 8, 6, 4, 10]\), compute the 5‑point simple moving average. Which points are missing? Plot roughly by hand.
  2. Explain why an even‑length moving average uses half‑weights. What would happen if we used a simple average of 2 points without adjustment?
  3. When we estimate the trend with a 12‑month moving average for monthly data, seasonal fluctuations are removed. Why does this work? (Think about the period of seasonality.)

2.2 Linear Filters & Convolution

A linear filter is defined by coefficients \(a_{-q},\dots,a_q\): \(w_t = \sum_{j=-q}^q a_j x_{t-j}\). This is the convolution \(\mathbf{a} * \mathbf{x}\).

Worked Example 2.2 – Filter Convolution

Let \(\mathbf{a} = (\frac14, \frac12, \frac14)\) and \(\mathbf{x} = (10, 20, 30, 40)\). (\(a_{-1}=\frac14, a_0=\frac12, a_1=\frac14\)). Compute \(w_t\) for \(t=2,3\).

\(w_2 = a_{-1}x_3 + a_0 x_2 + a_1 x_1 = \frac14\cdot30 + \frac12\cdot20 + \frac14\cdot10 = 7.5+10+2.5 = 20\).
\(w_3 = a_{-1}x_4 + a_0 x_3 + a_1 x_2 = \frac14\cdot40 + \frac12\cdot30 + \frac14\cdot20 = 10+15+5 = 30\).

Exercises – Filters

  1. For the filter coefficients \(a = [0.2, 0.6, 0.2]\), apply to \(x = [5, 7, 9, 8]\). Calculate all possible output values (ignore boundaries or use zero padding).
  2. Check whether the filter \(a = [0.2, 0.6, 0.2]\) preserves a constant trend (i.e., \(m_t = c\)). Compute \(\sum a_j\) and \(\sum j a_j\). Does it preserve a linear trend?

2.3 Filter Design Conditions

To pass a polynomial trend of degree \(d\) without distortion:

  • \(\sum a_j = 1\)
  • \(\sum j a_j = 0\) (linear)
  • \(\sum j^2 a_j = 0\) (quadratic), etc.

Worked Example 2.3 – Spencer’s 15‑point Filter (Verification)

Spencer’s filter is symmetric with \(a_0=74/320, a_1=67/320, \dots\). By construction it satisfies the three summation conditions, so cubic trends pass through unchanged. (Verify numerically that \(\sum a_j = 1\) and \(\sum j^2 a_j = 0\).)

Chapter 3 – Non‑parametric Trend Methods II: Exponential Smoothing and Differencing

3.1 Exponential Smoothing

Infinite weighted average: \(m_t = \alpha x_t + \alpha(1-\alpha)x_{t-1} + \alpha(1-\alpha)^2 x_{t-2} + \dots\)

Recurrence: \(m_t = \alpha x_t + (1-\alpha) m_{t-1}\), with \(m_0 = x_0\).

Weights sum to 1. The span (average age) is \(N = (1-\alpha)/\alpha\).

Worked Example 3.1 – Exponential Smoothing by Hand

Data: \(x = [20, 22, 19, 23]\); start \(m_1 = x_1 = 20\); \(\alpha = 0.4\).
\(m_2 = 0.4\times22 + 0.6\times20 = 8.8 + 12 = 20.8\)
\(m_3 = 0.4\times19 + 0.6\times20.8 = 7.6 + 12.48 = 20.08\)
\(m_4 = 0.4\times23 + 0.6\times20.08 = 9.2 + 12.048 = 21.248\)

Forecast for \(t=5\) would be \(m_4 = 21.248\).

Exercises – Exponential Smoothing

  2. Given a desired span of \(N=10\) time points, what \(\alpha\) should you use? Verify that for \(\alpha=1/(N+1)\) the weights sum to 1 (finite case, assuming an infinite past).
  3. Explain why exponential smoothing is a “one‑sided” method suitable for forecasting, while a symmetric moving average is not.

3.2 Holt’s Linear Trend Method

Level: \(m_t = \alpha x_t + (1-\alpha)(m_{t-1}+b_{t-1})\)
Trend: \(b_t = \beta (m_t - m_{t-1}) + (1-\beta)b_{t-1}\)
Forecast: \(\hat x_{t+h} = m_t + h\,b_t\).

Worked Example 3.2 – Holt Method

Data: \(x_1=10, x_2=12, x_3=11, x_4=14\). Initial values: \(m_1=x_1=10,\; b_1=1\). \(\alpha=0.5,\; \beta=0.5\).
\(t=2\): \(m_2 = 0.5\times12 + 0.5\times(10+1) = 6 + 5.5 = 11.5\); \(b_2 = 0.5(11.5-10) + 0.5\times1 = 0.75+0.5=1.25\).
\(t=3\): \(m_3 = 0.5\times11 + 0.5\times(11.5+1.25) = 5.5 + 6.375 = 11.875\); \(b_3 = 0.5(11.875-11.5)+0.5\times1.25 = 0.1875+0.625=0.8125\).
Forecast \(t=5\): \(\hat x_5 = m_4 + 1\cdot b_4\) (need \(m_4,b_4\) first). Continue to see the trend adaptation.

Exercises – Holt Method

  1. Continue the example to obtain \(m_4, b_4\) and forecast \(x_5\).
  2. Describe in words the difference between the roles of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). What happens if \(\beta=0\)?

3.3 Trend Differencing

Operator: \(\nabla x_t = x_t - x_{t-1} = (I-B)x_t\).
\(\nabla^d\) removes polynomial trend of degree \(d\).

Worked Example 3.3 – Differencing

Data with linear trend: \(x = [3, 5, 7, 9, 11]\) (true \(m_t = 1+2t\)).
\(\nabla x = [2, 2, 2, 2]\) – constant, trend removed.

If quadratic: \(x = [1, 4, 9, 16, 25]\) (\(m_t = t^2\)). \(\nabla x = [3, 5, 7, 9]\); still linear. \(\nabla^2 x = [2, 2, 2]\) – constant.

Exercises – Differencing

  1. Given \(x = [2, 6, 12, 20, 30]\), apply \(\nabla\) and \(\nabla^2\). What kind of trend was present?
  2. If a time series has a seasonal pattern of period 12 and a linear trend, which differencing combination(s) would remove both? Explain why.

Chapter 4 – Seasonality

4.1 Seasonal Component

Periodic with period \(S\): \(s_{t+S}=s_t\). Often \(\sum_{t=1}^S s_t = 0\).

4.2 Brockwell & Davies Decomposition (Steps)

  1. Estimate trend by \(S\)-point moving average.
  2. Detrended series: \(x_t - \hat m_t\).
  3. Average detrended values for each season → raw seasonal indices \(w_k\).
  4. Centre: \(\hat s_k = w_k - \frac1S\sum w_i\).
  5. Deseasonalised: \(d_t = x_t - \hat s_t\); re‑estimate trend if desired.
  6. Residuals: \(\hat y_t = x_t - \hat m_t - \hat s_t\).

Worked Example 4.1 – Quarterly Data (Brockwell‑Davies)

Consider \(x\) with \(n=8\) quarters (2 years). (As in the lecture notes exercise with numbers provided.)

\(S=4\). Trend \(m_t\) = 4‑point even moving average. Compute first \(m_3\): \[ m_3 = \frac{x_1/2 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4 + x_5/2}{4}. \] Then detrend, average Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4 values, centre, and subtract. The resulting seasonal indices are periodic. Residuals should be stationary. (Full manual calculation is a good practice.)

Exercises – Seasonal Decomposition

  1. For the series given in the notes (32 values), set up a spreadsheet or calculate by hand the first two trend values (even moving average) and the raw seasonal factors for Q1 and Q2.
  2. Explain why the moving average length must equal the seasonal period \(S\) to extract seasonality. What would happen if we used a length shorter than \(S\)?

4.3 Holt‑Winters with Seasonality

\[ \begin{aligned} m_t &= \alpha (x_t - s_{t-S}) + (1-\alpha)(m_{t-1}+b_{t-1}),\\ b_t &= \beta (m_t - m_{t-1}) + (1-\beta)b_{t-1},\\ s_t &= \gamma (x_t - m_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + (1-\gamma)s_{t-S}. \end{aligned} \]

Worked Example 4.2 – Holt‑Winters (simplified)

Take monthly data (\(S=12\)) and suppose initial level \(m_0=100\), trend \(b_0=2\), and initial seasonal factors \(s_{-11},\dots,s_0\) all 0. With \(\alpha=0.3,\beta=0.1,\gamma=0.2\), and a new observation \(x_1=105\), compute one‑step update.
\(m_1 = 0.3(105-0)+0.7(100+2) = 31.5+71.4=102.9\),
\(b_1 = 0.1(102.9-100)+0.9(2) = 0.29+1.8=2.09\),
\(s_1 = 0.2(105-100-2)+0.8(0) = 0.2\times3 = 0.6\).

Exercises – Holt‑Winters

  1. Why is the term \(x_t - s_{t-S}\) used in the level equation? What does it achieve compared to the plain Holt method?
  2. If the seasonal pattern is very stable, would you choose \(\gamma\) close to 0 or close to 1? Explain.

4.4 Seasonal Differencing

\[ \nabla_S x_t = x_t - x_{t-S} = (I-B^S)x_t. \]

\(\nabla\) and \(\nabla_S\) commute. Combined \(\nabla\nabla_S\) removes linear trend + seasonality.

Worked Example 4.3 – Seasonal Differencing

Monthly data with yearly seasonality: \(x_{\text{Jan}} = 10, 12, 14\) over three years. Then \(\nabla_{12} x_{\text{Jan, year 2}} = 12-10 = 2\). The seasonal component is eliminated, leaving a possible linear trend (difference constant 2). If we additionally apply \(\nabla\), we get \(\nabla\nabla_{12} x = 0\).

4.5 Harmonic Decomposition

\[ s_t = a_0 + \sum_{j=1}^k \bigl( a_j \cos(2\pi j t / S) + b_j \sin(2\pi j t / S) \bigr). \]

Use linear regression to estimate \(a_j,b_j\). Useful for multiple periodicities.

Exercises – Harmonic Decomposition

  1. Suppose you have hourly temperature data with both daily (\(S=24\)) and weekly (\(S=168\)) cycles. How would you set up the harmonic model?
  2. Why might we choose \(k < S/2\) instead of using all possible frequencies?

Chapter 5 – Analysis of the Residual Error

5.1 Heteroskedasticity and Box‑Cox Transformation

\[ x_t^{(\lambda)} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x_t^\lambda -1}{\lambda}, & \lambda \neq 0,\\ \log x_t, & \lambda = 0. \end{cases} \]

Choose \(\lambda\) via maximum likelihood. A log transform (\(\lambda=0\)) is the most common variance‑stabilising technique.

Worked Example 5.1 – Box‑Cox

Data: \(x = [10, 12, 9, 20, 50, 200]\) (increasing variance).
Try log transform: \(\log x \approx [2.30, 2.48, 2.20, 3.00, 3.91, 5.30]\). The spread is more uniform.

5.2 Probability Essentials

Expectation, Variance, Covariance, Correlation

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum x_i p_i,\quad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2],\quad \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)(Y-\mathbb{E}Y)], \] \[ \rho_{XY} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)\operatorname{Var}(Y)}}. \]

Partial Correlation

\(\rho_{XY\cdot Z}\) is the correlation between \(X' = X - aZ\) and \(Y' = Y - bZ\) where \(a,b\) chosen so that \(\operatorname{Cov}(X',Z)=\operatorname{Cov}(Y',Z)=0\).

Worked Example 5.2 – Partial Correlation

Let \(X = 2Z + \varepsilon_1\), \(Y = 3Z + \varepsilon_2\), with \(Z,\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2\) all independent, mean 0, variances 1. Then \(\rho_{XY} = 6/\sqrt{5\cdot10} = 0.848\).
Remove \(Z\): \(X' = X - 2Z = \varepsilon_1\), \(Y' = Y - 3Z = \varepsilon_2\), so \(\rho_{XY\cdot Z} = 0\).

Exercises – Correlation & Partial Correlation

  2. Give a real‑life example where a high correlation between two variables is entirely explained by a third variable, and partial correlation would be near zero.

5.3 Weak Stationarity

A process \(\{X_t\}\) is weakly stationary if:

  1. \(\mathbb{E}[X_t] = \mu\) constant.
  2. \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}) = \gamma(h)\) depends only on lag \(h\).

Exercise – Stationarity

  2. Give an example of a process that has constant mean but non‑constant variance (thus not weakly stationary).

Chapter 6 – Properties of Stationary Processes

6.1 White Noise

\(Z_t \sim \text{iid } N(0,\sigma^2)\). ACF: \(\rho(0)=1,\; \rho(h)=0\) for \(h\neq 0\).

6.2 Sample ACF

Given data \(x_1,\dots,x_n\):

\[ \hat\gamma(h) = \frac1n \sum_{t=1}^{n-h} (x_{t+h}-\bar x)(x_t - \bar x),\quad \hat\rho(h) = \frac{\hat\gamma(h)}{\hat\gamma(0)}. \]

For white noise, \(\hat\rho(h) \stackrel{approx}{\sim} N(0, 1/n)\). 95% confidence band: \(\pm 1.96/\sqrt{n}\).

Worked Example 6.1 – Sample ACF

Small data: \(x = [2, -1, 0, 1, -2]\). \(\bar x = 0\). \(\hat\gamma(0) = \frac15(2^2+(-1)^2+0^2+1^2+(-2)^2) = \frac{10}{5}=2\).
\(\hat\gamma(1) = \frac15[(2)(-1)+(-1)(0)+(0)(1)+(1)(-2)] = \frac15[-2+0+0-2] = -0.8\).
\(\hat\rho(1) = -0.8/2 = -0.4\).

Exercises – Sample ACF

  1. For \(x = [3, 0, -3, 0, 3]\), compute \(\hat\rho(1)\) and \(\hat\rho(2)\). Do they resemble white noise?
  2. If \(n=100\), the critical value for ACF is about \(0.196\). If a sample ACF shows \(\hat\rho(1)=0.25\), \(\hat\rho(2)=0.18\), \(\hat\rho(5)=0.22\), what might you conclude?

6.3 MA(1) Process

\[ X_t = Z_t + \theta Z_{t-1}. \]

ACF: \(\rho(1)=\frac{\theta}{1+\theta^2}\), zero for higher lags.

Worked Example 6.2 – MA(1) ACF

Suppose \(\theta = 0.6\). Then \(\rho(1) = 0.6/(1+0.36) = 0.441\). Simulated sample ACF should show a significant spike at lag 1 and nothing else.

Exercises – MA(1)

  2. If \(\theta = -0.8\), what is \(\rho(1)\)? What does a negative \(\theta\) imply about the relationship between adjacent observations?

6.4 Stationarity Tests

ADF test: \(H_0\): non‑stationary (unit root). Reject if p‑value < 0.05 → series is stationary.
KPSS test: \(H_0\): stationary.
Ljung‑Box test: \(H_0\): white noise (no autocorrelation).

Worked Example 6.3 – Ljung‑Box Test

For the monsoon data in the notes, Box.test gave p‑value 0.2758 > 0.05, so we fail to reject the null of white noise. That suggests the series may be white noise.

Chapter 7 – Moving Average and Autoregressive Models

7.1 MA(\(q\)) Process

\[ X_t = Z_t + \theta_1 Z_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q Z_{t-q}. \]

ACVF: \(\gamma(h) = \sigma^2 \sum_{i=0}^{q-|h|} \theta_i \theta_{i+|h|}\), \(\theta_0=1\). Zero for \(|h|>q\).

Worked Example 7.1 – MA(2) ACF

\(\theta_1=0.5,\; \theta_2=-0.3\). Then: \(\gamma(0)=\sigma^2(1+0.5^2+(-0.3)^2)=1.34\sigma^2\), \(\gamma(1)=\sigma^2(0.5 + 0.5\times(-0.3)) = 0.35\sigma^2\), \(\gamma(2)=\sigma^2(-0.3)= -0.3\sigma^2\). ACF: \(\rho(1)=0.35/1.34\approx 0.261\), \(\rho(2)=-0.3/1.34\approx -0.224\).

Exercises – MA(q)

7.2 AR(1) Model

\[ X_t = \phi X_{t-1} + Z_t. \]

Causal if \(|\phi|<1\). Then \(\rho(h) = \phi^{|h|}\).

Worked Example 7.2 – AR(1) ACF

\(\phi=0.7\). Then theoretical ACF: \(\rho(1)=0.7,\; \rho(2)=0.49,\; \rho(3)=0.343,\dots\)

  3. Why is the AR(1) process with \(|\phi|>1\) considered non‑causal? Write the representation in terms of future innovations.

7.3 Causality and Invertibility

Causal: process depends only on past white noise.
Invertible: white noise can be reconstructed from past and present observations.

Chapter 8 – Autoregressive Processes of Order \(p\)

8.1 AR(\(p\)) Yule‑Walker Equations

\[ \gamma(0) = \phi_1\gamma(1)+\dots+\phi_p\gamma(p) + \sigma^2, \] \[ \gamma(h) = \phi_1\gamma(h-1)+\dots+\phi_p\gamma(h-p), \quad h>0. \]

8.2 Solving the Recurrence – AR(2) Example

Assume \(X_t = \phi_1 X_{t-1} + \phi_2 X_{t-2} + Z_t\). The ACF satisfies \(\rho(h) = \phi_1 \rho(h-1) + \phi_2 \rho(h-2)\).

With \(\rho(0)=1\) and \(\rho(1) = \phi_1/(1-\phi_2)\). The general solution depends on the roots of \(\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \phi_2 z^2\).

Worked Example 8.1 – AR(2) ACF

Take \(\phi_1 = 0.5,\; \phi_2 = 0.3\). Roots of \(1-0.5z-0.3z^2=0\) are \(r_{1,2} \approx 1.26\) and \(-2.63\) (both >1 in modulus, so causal). Then \(\rho(1) = 0.5/(1-0.3) = 0.714\).
Recurrence: \(\rho(2) = 0.5\times0.714 + 0.3\times1 = 0.357+0.3=0.657\). \(\rho(3) = 0.5\times0.657 + 0.3\times0.714 = 0.3285+0.2142=0.5427\). The ACF decays.

Exercises – AR(p)

  1. For an AR(2) with \(\phi_1=0.8,\; \phi_2=-0.6\), compute \(\rho(1)\), \(\rho(2)\), \(\rho(3)\). Check if the process is causal (roots of \(\phi(z)\)).
  2. Write down the Yule‑Walker equations for an AR(3) process and identify how you would solve for the initial values.

8.3 Linear Recurrence Theory (Optional)

Order‑2 recurrence: \(a_n = A a_{n-1} + B a_{n-2}\). Characteristic equation \(r^2 - Ar - B = 0\). Solution \(a_n = \alpha r_1^n + \beta r_2^n\) if roots distinct.

Worked Example 8.2 – Fibonacci

Fibonacci: \(F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2}\), \(F_0=0,F_1=1\). Characteristic eq: \(r^2 - r -1 =0\), roots \(r_1=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}, r_2=\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}\). Then \(F_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}(r_1^n - r_2^n)\).

Chapter 9 – Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF)

9.1 Definition

The partial autocorrelation at lag \(h\), \(\phi_{hh}\), is the coefficient of \(X_{t-h}\) in the linear regression of \(X_t\) on \(X_{t-1},\dots,X_{t-h}\). Equivalently, the partial correlation after removing intermediate lags.

9.2 PACF Patterns

  • AR(\(p\)): \(\phi_{hh} = 0\) for \(h > p\).
  • MA(\(q\)): tails off gradually.

Worked Example 9.1 – AR(2) PACF

Simulate AR(2) with \(\phi_1=0.5,\phi_2=0.3\). The sample PACF will show significant spikes at lags 1 and 2, and near zero after that.

Exercises – PACF

  2. You are given the ACF and PACF plots of a time series. The ACF decays slowly and the PACF shows a single large spike at lag 1. What model would you tentatively identify?

Chapter 10 – Fitting and Forecasting

10.1 Fitting MA and AR Models

Use maximum likelihood (R: arima). Order specified as c(p, d, q); for stationary residual, \(d=0\).

Worked Example 10.1 – Fitting an AR(2)

After inspecting ACF/PACF, we fit arima(x, order=c(2,0,0)). Output gives \(\hat\phi_1, \hat\phi_2\), and \(\hat\sigma^2\). For example, \(\hat\phi_1=0.44, \hat\phi_2=0.23\).

10.2 Forecasting MA(\(q\))

At time \(t\), we know innovations up to \(Z_t\). One‑step: \(\hat X_{t+1} = \theta_1 Z_t + \dots + \theta_q Z_{t-q+1}\). For \(h>q\), forecast = process mean (0).

Worked Example 10.2 – MA(2) Forecast

Suppose fitted MA(2): \(X_t = Z_t + 0.5 Z_{t-1} - 0.3 Z_{t-2}\) and we have estimated innovations \(\hat Z_t = 1.2,\; \hat Z_{t-1}=0.8\). Then \(\hat X_{t+1} = 0.5\times1.2 + (-0.3)\times0.8 = 0.6 -0.24 = 0.36\). \(\hat X_{t+2} = -0.3\times1.2 = -0.36\). \(\hat X_{t+3}=0\).

10.3 Forecasting AR(\(p\))

Direct recursion: \(\hat X_{t+1} = \phi_1 X_t + \dots + \phi_p X_{t-p+1}\), and for \(h>1\) substitute forecasts for unknown past values.

Worked Example 10.3 – AR(2) Forecast

AR(2): \(X_t = 0.5 X_{t-1} + 0.3 X_{t-2} + Z_t\). Last two observed values: \(X_t = 10,\; X_{t-1}=8\). Then \(\hat X_{t+1} = 0.5\times10 + 0.3\times8 = 5+2.4=7.4\). \(\hat X_{t+2} = 0.5\times7.4 + 0.3\times10 = 3.7+3=6.7\).
