Chapter 0 – Introduction to Time Series

0.1 What is Time Series Analysis?

A time series is a sequence of data points collected at successive points in time, usually equally spaced. Examples: daily stock prices, monthly rainfall, yearly GDP.

Time Series Analysis (TSA) aims to:

  • Understand the underlying structure (trend, seasonality, cycles).
  • Explain the observed variation using mathematical models.
  • Predict future values (forecasting).
  • Control a process by adjusting inputs based on the model (e.g., in engineering).

0.2 What is a Time Series?

We observe a variable \(X\) at times \(t_1, t_2, \ldots\) and record numerical values \(x_1, x_2, \ldots\) In this course we assume: equispaced discrete time, i.e. \(t_2-t_1 = t_3-t_2 = \dots\), and often set \(t=1,2,3,\dots\) for simplicity. The data are continuous numbers (\(x_t\in\mathbb{R}\)) and contain random fluctuations.

0.3 How do we analyse Time Series?

We combine probability theory (modelling the randomness) and statistics (estimating model parameters from data).

Probability vs. Statistics:
  • Probability: We reason about random variables (usually upper case \(X, Y,\dots\)) and their distributions.
  • Statistics: We work with actual data (lower case \(x,y,\dots\)) to infer properties of the underlying random variables.

Chapter 1 – The Additive Model and Estimating the Trend

1.1 General Features of a Time Series

  • Trend (\(m_t\)) – a long‑term, smooth, non‑random movement (e.g., linear growth).
  • Seasonality (\(s_t\)) – regular periodic patterns with a known fixed period (e.g., monthly sales peaks in December).
  • Cyclic patterns – wavelike fluctuations with less predictable period (e.g., business cycles). Not covered in this course.
  • Residual noise (\(Y_t\)) – the erratic, unpredictable part left after removing trend and seasonality.
  • Heteroscedasticity – variance of the series changes over time (opposite: homoscedasticity).

1.2 The Additive Model

We decompose the observed time series \(X_t\) (or \(x_t\)) as:

\[ X_t = m_t + s_t + Y_t, \qquad t=1,\ldots,n. \] The trend \(m_t\) and seasonal part \(s_t\) are deterministic; the noise \(Y_t\) is random with \(\mathbb{E}[Y_t]=0\).
Why centre the components? If the seasonal averages are not zero, we can redefine \(m_t^{\text{new}} = m_t + \text{avg}(s_t)\) and \(s_t^{\text{new}} = s_t - \text{avg}(s_t)\) so that \(\sum_{t=1}^{S} s_t^{\text{new}}/S = 0\).

1.3 Multiplicative Model

For series where variability is proportional to the level (e.g., financial returns), we use:

\[ X_t = m_t \, s_t \, Y_t. \] Taking logarithms converts it to an additive model: \[ \log X_t = \log m_t + \log s_t + \log Y_t. \]

1.4 Trend Estimation – Parametric Methods

1.4.1 Linear Regression

Assume the trend is a straight line: \(m_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t\). We find the parameters that minimise the sum of squared residuals:

\[ S(\beta_0,\beta_1) = \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl(x_i - (\beta_0 + \beta_1 t_i)\bigr)^2. \]

Setting partial derivatives to zero gives the normal equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum x_i &= n\beta_0 + \beta_1 \sum t_i,\\ \sum t_i x_i &= \beta_0 \sum t_i + \beta_1 \sum t_i^2. \end{aligned} \]

Solving yields the well‑known formulas:

\[ \hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (t_i - \bar t)(x_i - \bar x)}{\sum_{i=1}^n (t_i - \bar t)^2},\qquad \hat\beta_0 = \bar x - \hat\beta_1\bar t. \]
Intuition: \(\hat\beta_1\) is the slope (change in \(x\) per unit time). \(\hat\beta_0\) is the value of the trend at time 0. The fitted line always passes through the point of means \((\bar t,\bar x)\).

1.4.2 Polynomial Regression

If the trend looks curved, we use a polynomial: \(m_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \beta_2 t^2 + \dots + \beta_k t^k\).

We write the model in matrix form. Let \(\mathbf{X} = (x_1,\ldots,x_n)^T\), \(\boldsymbol\beta = (\beta_0,\beta_1,\dots,\beta_k)^T\), and define the design matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) (size \(n\times (k+1)\)):

\[ \mathbf{W} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t_1 & t_1^2 & \cdots & t_1^k\\ 1 & t_2 & t_2^2 & \cdots & t_2^k\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 1 & t_n & t_n^2 & \cdots & t_n^k \end{pmatrix}. \]

The sum of squares becomes \(S(\boldsymbol\beta) = \|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\beta\|^2 = (\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\beta)^T(\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\beta)\).

Using matrix calculus (\(\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol\beta} \|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\beta\|^2 = -2\mathbf{W}^T(\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\beta)\)), we set the gradient to zero and obtain the normal equations:

\[ \mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{X}. \]

Provided \(\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W}\) is invertible (which requires at least \(k+1\) distinct time points), the solution is:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{W})^{-1}\mathbf{W}^T\mathbf{X}. \]
Intuition: This is a direct generalisation of fitting a straight line; instead of two parameters we estimate \(k+1\) parameters simultaneously. The matrix formula is exactly the same algebraic structure.

1.4.3 Other Parametric Trends

Exponential trend: \(m_t = \beta_0 e^{\beta_1 t}\) – appropriate for percentage growth.
Richards’ growth curve (generalised logistic): Used in modelling population or epidemic growth. These require numerical optimisation to fit.

Chapter 2 – Non‑parametric Trend Methods I: Moving Averages and Filters

2.1 Moving Average Smoothing

2.1.1 Simple Moving Average (Odd Window)

For a chosen integer \(q\), the \(2q+1\)-point moving average is:

\[ \hat m_t = \frac{1}{2q+1}\bigl(x_{t-q} + x_{t-q+1} + \dots + x_{t+q}\bigr). \]

This smooths the series by averaging over \(2q+1\) consecutive observations, centred at \(t\). The first and last \(q\) points are undefined (missing).

2.1.2 Even Moving Average

When an even number \(2q\) of points is needed (e.g., to remove seasonality of period 12), we weight the ends by 1/2:

\[ \hat m_t = \frac{1}{2q}\Bigl(\tfrac12 x_{t-q} + x_{t-q+1} + \dots + x_{t+q-1} + \tfrac12 x_{t+q}\Bigr). \]

2.2 Linear Filters and Convolution

A linear filter is a transformation \(w_t = \sum_{j=-q}^{q} a_j x_{t-j}\) where the vector \(\mathbf{a} = (a_{-q},\ldots,a_q)\) is called the filter coefficients. This is the convolution of \(\mathbf{a}\) with the series \(\mathbf{x}\).

Low‑pass filter: Suppresses rapid fluctuations (high frequencies) and retains slow changes (the trend). Moving averages are low‑pass filters.

High‑pass filter: Removes slow changes and leaves the fast noise. Example: \(y_t = x_t - \hat m_t\).

2.3 Filter Design

A filter passes a trend without distortion if \(m_t = \sum_j a_j m_{t-j}\) for that trend.

For a polynomial trend of degree \(d\), the necessary and sufficient conditions are:

  • \(\sum_j a_j = 1\) (preserves constant)
  • \(\sum_j j a_j = 0\) (preserves linear)
  • \(\sum_j j^2 a_j = 0\) (preserves quadratic), etc. up to \(\sum_j j^d a_j = 0\).
Example: The simple \(2q+1\) moving average has \(a_j = 1/(2q+1)\) and satisfies the first two conditions, so it passes linear trends undisturbed.

2.4 Well‑Known Low‑Pass Filters

  • Spencer’s 15‑point filter: Coefficients satisfy conditions up to cubic, so it preserves quadratic and cubic trends. Used by actuaries for mortality data.
  • Henderson’s filter: Also widely used in official statistics; designed to be optimal for trend estimation.

Chapter 3 – Non‑parametric Trend Methods II: Exponential Smoothing and Differencing

3.1 Exponential Smoothing

3.1.1 Definition

Choose a smoothing parameter \(\alpha \in (0,1)\). The exponentially smoothed trend is defined as a weighted average of all past observations with geometrically decaying weights:

\[ m_t = \alpha x_t + \alpha(1-\alpha) x_{t-1} + \alpha(1-\alpha)^2 x_{t-2} + \dots \]

For a finite series starting at \(x_0\), the last term is \((1-\alpha)^t \hat m_0\), often set \(\hat m_0 = x_0\).

Why “exponential”? The weights decrease by a constant factor \(1-\alpha\) each step back in time, mimicking an exponential decay.

3.1.2 Recurrence Form

\[ m_t = \alpha x_t + (1-\alpha) m_{t-1}, \] with initial condition \(m_0 = x_0\). This makes computation O(1) per new data point.

3.1.3 Forecasting

Since exponential smoothing only uses past data, it can be used for one‑step‑ahead forecasts: \(\hat x_{t+1} = m_t\). The optimal \(\alpha\) is chosen by minimising the sum of squared forecast errors over the historical data.

3.1.4 Span

The average age of the data in the estimate is \(N = (1-\alpha)/\alpha\). For a desired span \(N\), we set \(\alpha = 1/(N+1)\). In finance, this gives the \(N\)-day exponential moving average.

3.2 Holt Method (Exponential Smoothing with Trend)

When the series has a persistent upward/downward drift, we add a trend component \(b_t\):

\[ \begin{aligned} m_t &= \alpha x_t + (1-\alpha)(m_{t-1} + b_{t-1}),\\[2pt] b_t &= \beta (m_t - m_{t-1}) + (1-\beta) b_{t-1}. \end{aligned} \]

Here \(m_t\) is the level, \(b_t\) is the slope (trend). Forecasting \(h\) steps ahead: \(\hat x_{t+h} = m_t + h\,b_t\).

Intuition: The level equation now adds the previous trend to the previous level before blending with the new observation. The trend equation updates the slope based on the latest change in level, smoothed by \(\beta\).

3.3 Trend Differencing

3.3.1 The Differencing Operator

\[ \nabla x_t = x_t - x_{t-1} = (I - B)x_t, \] where \(B\) is the backshift operator: \(B x_t = x_{t-1}\).

3.3.2 Higher Order Differencing

\[ \nabla^d = (I - B)^d. \] For example, \(\nabla^2 x_t = x_t - 2x_{t-1} + x_{t-2}\).

Key property: If the trend is a polynomial of degree \(d\), \(m_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \dots + \beta_d t^d\), then applying \(\nabla^d\) eliminates the trend completely, leaving only \(\nabla^d Y_t\) (plus a constant \(d!\,\beta_d\)).

Example: Linear trend \(m_t = \beta_0+\beta_1 t\) → \(\nabla m_t = \beta_1\) (constant, no trend). Quadratic trend → \(\nabla^2 m_t = 2\beta_2\) (constant).

Chapter 4 – Seasonality

4.1 Seasonal Patterns

A seasonal component repeats every \(S\) time units: \(s_{t+S} = s_t\). For monthly data with yearly seasonality, \(S=12\).

We always assume the seasonal component averages to zero over one cycle: \(\frac{1}{S}\sum_{t=1}^{S} s_t = 0\).

4.2 Brockwell & Davies Decomposition Algorithm

This is a step‑by‑step method to separate a series \(x_t\) into \(m_t + s_t + y_t\). It assumes the number of observations \(n\) is a multiple of \(S\).

  1. Estimate trend by an \(S\)-point moving average. If \(S\) is even (e.g. 12), use the even moving average with half‑weighted ends so that seasonal fluctuations are averaged out.
  2. Detrended series: \(x_t - \hat m_t\). For each season (e.g. each January), compute the average of these detrended values. Call these raw seasonal indices \(w_1,\dots,w_S\).
  3. Centre the indices: \(\hat s_k = w_k - \frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S} w_i\), so they sum to zero.
  4. Deseasonalised series: \(d_t = x_t - \hat s_t\).
  5. Re‑estimate trend from the deseasonalised data (optional), and then compute residuals \(\hat y_t = x_t - \hat m_t - \hat s_t\).
Intuition: The algorithm first uses a moving average of length exactly equal to the seasonal period to obtain a smooth trend free of seasonal wiggles. Then it averages the leftover seasonal bumps across years to get a stable seasonal profile.

4.3 Holt‑Winters Method with Seasonality

This extends the Holt method by adding a seasonal component \(s_t\) that is updated via exponential smoothing:

\[ \begin{aligned} m_t &= \alpha (x_t - s_{t-S}) + (1-\alpha)(m_{t-1} + b_{t-1}),\\ b_t &= \beta (m_t - m_{t-1}) + (1-\beta) b_{t-1},\\ s_t &= \gamma (x_t - m_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + (1-\gamma) s_{t-S}. \end{aligned} \]

Forecast: \(\hat x_{t+h} = m_t + h\,b_t + s_{t - S + 1 + (h-1)\bmod S}\).

4.4 Seasonal Differencing

\[ \nabla_S x_t = x_t - x_{t-S} = (I - B^S) x_t. \]

Applying \(\nabla_S\) to a seasonal component of period \(S\) gives zero, thus removing seasonality. \(\nabla\) and \(\nabla_S\) commute, so the order of detrending and deseasonalising does not matter. Combined \(\nabla\nabla_S\) removes both a linear trend and a seasonal pattern.

4.5 Harmonic Decomposition

Seasonal cycles can be expressed as a sum of sines and cosines of frequencies \(j/S\):

\[ s_t = a_0 + \sum_{j=1}^k \bigl( a_j \cos(2\pi j t/S) + b_j \sin(2\pi j t/S) \bigr). \]

The coefficients are estimated by least squares. This is useful when several different cycles (e.g., weekly and yearly) are present.

Chapter 5 – Analysis of the Residual Error

5.1 Heteroskedasticity and Box‑Cox Transformations

Heteroskedasticity: \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t)\) changes with time. Stationary models require constant variance.

The Box‑Cox transformation is a family of power transforms indexed by \(\lambda\):

\[ x_t^{(\lambda)} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x_t^\lambda - 1}{\lambda}, & \lambda \neq 0,\\[6pt] \log x_t, & \lambda = 0. \end{cases} \]

The optimal \(\lambda\) is chosen by maximum likelihood (R: BoxCox.lambda). A log‑transform (\(\lambda=0\)) is the most common cure.

5.2 Revision of Probability Concepts

5.2.1 One‑Dimensional Random Variables

For a discrete r.v. \(X\) with probability mass function \(p_i = P(X=x_i)\), the expected value is \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum x_i p_i\) and variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2] = \sum x_i^2 p_i - (\mathbb{E}X)^2\).

For a continuous r.v. with density \(f_X(x)\), \(\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \int g(x)f_X(x)\,dx\).

5.2.2 Two‑Dimensional and Higher Dimensions

Joint distribution: \(f_{(X,Y)}(x,y)\). Marginal densities: \(f_X(x) = \int f_{(X,Y)}(x,y)\,dy\).

Covariance: \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)(Y-\mathbb{E}Y)] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}X\,\mathbb{E}Y\).

Correlation: \(\rho_{XY} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)\operatorname{Var}(Y)}} \in [-1,1]\).

Properties: Covariance is bilinear and symmetric. Uncorrelated does NOT imply independent (except for jointly normal vectors). For a bivariate normal, zero correlation means independence.

5.2.3 Partial Correlation

The partial correlation \(\rho_{XY\cdot Z}\) is the correlation between \(X\) and \(Y\) after removing the linear effect of \(Z\). Formally, we find \(a,b\) such that \(\operatorname{Cov}(X - aZ, Z)=0\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(Y - bZ, Z)=0\); then \(\rho_{XY\cdot Z} = \rho_{X',Y'}\) where \(X' = X-aZ,\; Y' = Y-bZ\).

Intuition: If height and reading ability are both correlated with age, partial correlation removes the age effect, revealing whether height still predicts reading ability directly.

5.3 Random Processes and Weak Stationarity

A random process is a sequence of random variables \(\{X_t\}\). Its mean function is \(\mu(t) = \mathbb{E}[X_t]\) and its autocovariance function is:

\[ \gamma(r,s) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_r, X_s) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[(X_r-\mu(r))(X_s-\mu(s))\bigr]. \]

A process is weakly stationary if:

  1. \(\mu(t) = \mu\) (constant) for all \(t\).
  2. \(\gamma(t+h, t) = \gamma(h)\) depends only on the lag \(h\), not on \(t\).

Chapter 6 – Properties of Stationary Processes

6.1 Strict vs. Weak Stationarity

A process is strictly stationary if the whole joint distribution of \((X_{t_1},\dots,X_{t_n})\) is the same as that of \((X_{t_1+s},\dots,X_{t_n+s})\) for any shift \(s\).

If a strictly stationary process has finite first and second moments, then it is also weakly stationary (but the converse may not hold).

6.2 White Noise

White noise is a sequence of independent random variables, each with mean 0 and constant variance \(\sigma^2\), usually normal: \(Z_t \sim N(0,\sigma^2)\).

Its ACF: \(\rho(0)=1\), \(\rho(h)=0\) for \(|h|>0\). It is the simplest stationary process and the building block for more complex models.

6.3 Random Walk

\[ X_t = X_{t-1} + Z_t, \quad X_0 = Z_0. \]

Then \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t) = t\sigma^2\), so the variance depends on \(t\) → not stationary.

6.4 Autocovariance and Autocorrelation Functions (ACVF & ACF)

For a weakly stationary process, we define:

\[ \gamma(h) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t+h}),\qquad \rho(h) = \frac{\gamma(h)}{\gamma(0)}. \]

Properties: \(\gamma(0) \ge 0\), \(\rho(0)=1\), \(\gamma(-h)=\gamma(h)\), \(|\rho(h)| \le 1\).

6.5 Sample ACF

Given observations \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), the sample mean is \(\bar x = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^n x_t\).

The sample autocovariance:

\[ \hat\gamma(h) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n-h} (x_{t+h} - \bar x)(x_t - \bar x), \quad 0\le h < n. \]

The sample ACF is \(\hat\rho(h) = \hat\gamma(h) / \hat\gamma(0)\).

For white noise, \(\hat\rho(h)\) is approximately normally distributed with mean 0 and variance \(1/n\). The 95% confidence interval is \(\pm 1.96/\sqrt{n}\) (the blue dashed lines in R’s acf plot).

6.6 MA(1) Process

Definition: \(X_t = Z_t + \theta Z_{t-1}\).

Mean: \(\mathbb{E}[X_t]=0\). Covariances:

\[ \gamma(0) = \sigma^2(1+\theta^2),\quad \gamma(1) = \gamma(-1) = \theta\sigma^2,\quad \gamma(h)=0 \text{ for } |h|>1. \]

Hence ACF: \(\rho(1) = \frac{\theta}{1+\theta^2},\; \rho(h)=0\) for \(|h|>1\).

Memory: The effect of a shock lasts exactly one time step. The ACF cuts off after lag 1.

6.7 Stationarity Tests

  • ADF (Augmented Dickey‑Fuller): \(H_0\) = non‑stationary. Small p‑value → reject \(H_0\) → series is stationary.
  • KPSS: \(H_0\) = stationary.
  • Ljung‑Box: \(H_0\) = white noise (no autocorrelation).

Chapter 7 – Moving Average and Autoregressive Models

7.1 Moving Average Model of Order \(q\), MA(\(q\))

Definition:

\[ X_t = Z_t + \theta_1 Z_{t-1} + \theta_2 Z_{t-2} + \dots + \theta_q Z_{t-q}. \] Using the backshift operator, \(X_t = \theta(B) Z_t\) where \(\theta(z) = 1 + \theta_1 z + \dots + \theta_q z^q\).

ACVF of MA(\(q\)):

\[ \gamma(h) = \sigma^2 \sum_{i=0}^{q-|h|} \theta_i \theta_{i+|h|}, \quad \text{with } \theta_0 = 1. \]

Thus \(\gamma(h) = 0\) for \(|h| > q\). The ACF cuts off after lag \(q\).

Derivation: Using bilinearity of covariance and independence of white noise terms, the only non‑zero covariances occur when the subscripts of \(Z\) coincide.

7.2 AR(1) Model

Equation: \(X_t = \phi X_{t-1} + Z_t\), or \(\phi(B) X_t = Z_t\) with \(\phi(z) = 1 - \phi z\).

Existence of stationary solution: If \(|\phi| < 1\) (causal case), we can unroll the recursion:

\[ X_t = Z_t + \phi Z_{t-1} + \phi^2 Z_{t-2} + \dots \]

This infinite sum converges because the variances sum to \(\sigma^2/(1-\phi^2) < \infty\).

ACVF and ACF for \(|\phi|<1\):

\[ \gamma(0) = \frac{\sigma^2}{1-\phi^2},\qquad \gamma(h) = \phi^{|h|} \gamma(0),\qquad \rho(h) = \phi^{|h|}. \]
Causal meaning: The process is expressed entirely in terms of past innovations \(Z_t\). The ACF decays exponentially.

If \(|\phi| > 1\), we can write \(X_t\) in terms of future innovations (non‑causal). Its ACF is \(\rho(h) = \phi^{-|h|}\). To avoid ambiguity, we usually choose the causal representation.

7.3 Causality and Invertibility

  • Causal process: \(X_t\) can be expressed solely as a function of past white noise terms (i.e., \(Z_t, Z_{t-1},\dots\)).
  • Invertible process: The white noise \(Z_t\) can be recovered from present and past values of \(X_t\).

MA(\(q\)) processes are always causal. They are invertible iff all roots of \(\theta(z)\) lie outside the unit circle.

AR(1) is causal iff \(|\phi| < 1\); it is always invertible. For AR(\(p\)), causality holds iff all roots of \(\phi(z)\) lie outside the unit circle.

Chapter 8 – Autoregressive Processes of Order \(p\)

8.1 AR(\(p\)) Definition

\[ X_t - \phi_1 X_{t-1} - \phi_2 X_{t-2} - \dots - \phi_p X_{t-p} = Z_t, \] or \(\phi(B) X_t = Z_t\) with \(\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \dots - \phi_p z^p\).

8.2 Yule–Walker Equations

For a causal AR(\(p\)) process, we multiply the defining equation by \(X_{t-h}\) and take expectations. Using \(\mathbb{E}[Z_t X_{t-h}] = \sigma^2\) only when \(h=0\) (due to causality), we get:

\[ \gamma(0) = \phi_1 \gamma(1) + \dots + \phi_p \gamma(p) + \sigma^2, \] \[ \gamma(h) = \phi_1 \gamma(h-1) + \dots + \phi_p \gamma(h-p), \qquad h > 0. \]

These are linear recurrence equations for the autocovariances. The ACF satisfies the same recurrence with \(\rho(0)=1\).

8.3 Solving the Recurrence – General Solution (Optional but Important)

The recurrence \(a_n = \phi_1 a_{n-1} + \dots + \phi_p a_{n-p}\) has characteristic polynomial:

\[ p(z) = z^p - \phi_1 z^{p-1} - \dots - \phi_p. \] But it is more convenient to work with the original \(\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \dots - \phi_p z^p\). The roots of \(\phi(z)\), say \(r_1,\dots,r_p\) (all \(|r_i| > 1\) for causality), are the inverses of the characteristic roots.

The general solution for the ACF is:

\[ \rho(h) = \alpha_1 r_1^{-h} + \alpha_2 r_2^{-h} + \dots + \alpha_p r_p^{-h}, \] where the constants \(\alpha_i\) are determined by the initial values \(\rho(0)=1, \rho(1), \dots, \rho(p-1)\).

Why this form? Each term \((1/r_i)^{-h} = r_i^{-h}\) is an exponential decay if \(|r_i|>1\). Complex roots produce damped sinusoids.

Example: AR(2)

For \(X_t = \phi_1 X_{t-1} + \phi_2 X_{t-2} + Z_t\), the recurrence for \(\rho(h)\) is \(\rho(h) = \phi_1 \rho(h-1) + \phi_2 \rho(h-2)\). The initial values are \(\rho(0)=1\) and \(\rho(1) = \phi_1/(1-\phi_2)\) (obtained from the recurrence at \(h=1\) with \(\rho(-1)=\rho(1)\)).

If the roots \(r_1, r_2\) of \(\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1 z - \phi_2 z^2\) are real and distinct, then:

\[ \rho(h) = \frac{r_2 - 1}{r_2 - r_1} r_1^{-h} + \frac{1 - r_1}{r_2 - r_1} r_2^{-h}. \]

If the roots are complex conjugates, \(\rho(h)\) oscillates while decaying.

Chapter 9 – The Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF)

9.1 Motivation

For an AR(1) process, \(\rho(2) \neq 0\) even though \(X_t\) depends directly only on \(X_{t-1}\). The correlation at lag 2 is “inherited” through lag 1. The partial autocorrelation eliminates the intermediate lags’ influence.

9.2 Definition

The partial autocorrelation at lag \(h\), denoted \(\phi_{hh}\), is the partial correlation between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-h}\) after removing the linear effects of \(X_{t-1}, X_{t-2}, \dots, X_{t-h+1}\).

Formally: \(\phi_{hh}\) equals the last coefficient in the linear regression of \(X_t\) on \(X_{t-1},\dots,X_{t-h}\).

9.3 PACF of AR and MA Processes

  • AR(\(p\)): \(\phi_{hh} = 0\) for all \(h > p\). The PACF cuts off after lag \(p\).
  • MA(\(q\)): The PACF decays gradually (tails off).
  • AR(1): \(\phi_{11} = \phi\), and \(\phi_{hh}=0\) for \(h>1\).

Identification strategy: Plot sample ACF and PACF. If ACF has a sharp drop after lag \(q\) and PACF decays slowly, choose MA(\(q\)). If PACF drops after lag \(p\) and ACF decays, choose AR(\(p\)).

Chapter 10 – Fitting and Forecasting (Optional)

10.1 Fitting Models in R

Once a stationary residual series is obtained (after detrending and deseasonalising), we fit models using maximum likelihood via arima.

  • MA(\(q\)): arima(x, order = c(0,0,q))
  • AR(\(p\)): arima(x, order = c(p,0,0))

The output gives estimated coefficients, their standard errors, and the innovation variance \(\hat\sigma^2\).

10.2 Forecasting

10.2.1 Forecasting with MA(\(q\))

Assume we are at time \(t\) and know the past innovations. For a one‑step forecast:

\[ \hat X_{t+1} = \theta_1 Z_t + \theta_2 Z_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q Z_{t+1-q}. \]

For \(h>q\), the forecast is simply the process mean (zero, since we work with centred series). In practice, the innovations \(Z_t\) are not directly observed but can be estimated recursively using the invertibility of the model.

10.2.2 Forecasting with AR(\(p\))

The forecast is straightforward: plug in the most recent observations (or previous forecasts) into the difference equation:

\[ \hat X_{t+1} = \phi_1 X_t + \dots + \phi_p X_{t-p+1} \] \[ \hat X_{t+2} = \phi_1 \hat X_{t+1} + \phi_2 X_t + \dots + \phi_p X_{t-p+2},\quad \text{etc.} \]
R implementation: After fitting with arima, use predict(model, n.ahead = h) to obtain forecasts and confidence intervals.
